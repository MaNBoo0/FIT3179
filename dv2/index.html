Forever Friends?

What decides how long a pet lives, and what Australian owners can do to keep theirs healthy.

Chapter 1

Lifespans differ by species

How long will a new pet share your life? The species decides most of the answer. Among 23 common pets, the longest recorded lives run from under 4 years for a rat to 57 for a sulphur-crested cockatoo.

These are records: the oldest animal known, usually one kept in captivity. Most pets fall well short of their record, but the order shows what each species can reach.

30 yearsthe oldest cat on record
27 yearsthe oldest dog on record
Birds top the list

The sulphur-crested cockatoo's record is 57 years, the galah's 40.

Cats and dogs sit in the middle

The oldest cat on record reached 30, the oldest dog 27.

Small mammals are shortest

Rats, mice and hamsters have records of about 4 years.

Not only birds

A goldfish's record is 41 years and a snake-necked turtle's 37, longer than any cat or dog.

For owners

Several of the longest-lived pets are native birds: the sulphur-crested cockatoo (57 years), galah (40) and cockatiel (35). A parrot bought today could outlive its owner, so plan who will care for it.

Heavier lives longer

A 20 g mouse's record is 4 years; a 300 kg horse's is 57.

Birds break the rule

A 30 g budgerigar can reach 21 years, five times as long as a mouse of similar size.

Cats and dogs sit close together

A 4 kg cat's record (30 years) is close to a 40 kg dog's (27), though the dog is ten times heavier.

Dogs break the size rule the other way, as the next chapter shows.

Chapter 2

Within dogs, bigger and flatter means shorter

Across species, bigger animals live longer. Dogs turn that rule upside down: small breeds outlive large ones by about two years, and a flat face costs years at any size.

Size still counts

For owners

If a long life together matters to you, start with a small or medium breed without a flat face.

Chapter 3

Will my pet see its tenth birthday?

Dogs and cats die at similar ages on average, but they get there differently. Cats are more likely to die young, yet those that make it often live years longer than dogs.

For owners

Ask your vet about desexing, especially for a male cat. This study shows a link, not proof: desexed pets may also get more care in other ways.

Chapter 4

Australia is choosing differently

Half of Australian homes have a dog and a third have a cat. Among pedigree dogs, tastes are shifting: Australians are turning away from the bulldog breeds, and the Golden Retriever has risen to number one.

Ranks are based on pedigree puppies registered with Dogs Australia, 2018 to 2024, not every dog bought.

Chapter 5

Local dangers

Some dangers depend on where you live. The paralysis tick lives along Australia's east coast and is most active in spring. Cane toads, poisonous to dogs that mouth them, have spread from Queensland across the Top End and into Western Australia.

The maps show records in the Atlas of Living Australia, so they reflect where people looked as well as where the animals live. Most tick records are photos shared since 2015.

Chapter 6

When care breaks down

Not every pet stays home. In 2024–25 the RSPCA took in 15,852 dogs and 25,639 cats. Most found new homes, but lost cats rarely went back to their owners, and illness ended far more cat lives in some states than in others.
